Your spot, going around the axis

Earth turns once every 23 h 56 m 4 s. Everyone completes that circle in the same time — but the circle is a very different size depending on how far you are from the axis. At the equator you sweep out 40,000 km. Standing exactly on a pole, you just pivot.

The whole planet, going around the Sun

This one is the same for everybody, and it is roughly 64 times faster than the spin. The orbit below is drawn true to shape, which is the surprise: it really is that close to a perfect circle. The Sun is off-centre by 2.5 million km — a huge number that still amounts to about two pixels here. Earth speeds up when it is closer in and slows down when it is further out, and that variation is far easier to see on the graph than in the picture.

How these numbers are worked out

Spin. Earth is a slightly squashed sphere, so the distance from the spin axis at your latitude uses the WGS-84 ellipsoid rather than a plain sphere. Speed is that distance times 2π, divided by the sidereal day of 86,164.09 s — the time to turn once relative to the stars, which is about 4 minutes shorter than a solar day.

Orbit. Position comes from solving Kepler's equation for the date, using a semi-major axis of 1.4960 × 108 km and an eccentricity of 0.0167. Speed then comes from the vis-viva equation, v = √(GM(2/r − 1/a)). That gives 30.29 km/s at perihelion in early January and 29.29 km/s at aphelion in early July.

Honest caveats. The orbit drawing is true to shape — it really is that close to a circle, and the Sun really is that slightly off-centre. The globe and orbit animations are hugely sped up (the factors are shown on screen), but the distance odometer at the top counts real elapsed time at real speed. Both figures ignore the Solar System's own motion around the galaxy, which is another 720,000 km/h or so on top.
